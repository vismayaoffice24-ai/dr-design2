Kidney Stones: Causes & Prevention

A nephrologist's clinical guide to understanding urinary crystal supersaturation, metabolic risk factors, and stopping stone recurrence.

Kidney stones (nephrolithiasis) are one of the most painful urological emergencies a person can encounter. Characterized by severe, cramping flank pain radiating to the lower abdomen and groin, stones affect millions each year.

While treating an active obstructive stone often requires urological relief, managing the underlying biochemical disorder that caused the stone to form in the first place falls under the medical domain of clinical nephrology. Without comprehensive metabolic investigation, over 50% of stone formers develop another stone within five to ten years.

Why Do Kidney Stones Form?

Urine naturally contains waste minerals dissolved in liquid. When fluid volume is low and the concentration of mineral salts—most commonly calcium, oxalate, uric acid, and phosphate—becomes excessively high, the urine becomes supersaturated.

Under supersaturated conditions, microscopic crystals precipitate out of solution, adhere to the renal papillary surface, and aggregate into solid stone concretions over weeks or months. The most common stone varieties include:

  • Calcium Oxalate & Calcium Phosphate Stones: Representing roughly 75% to 80% of all cases.
  • Uric Acid Stones: Linked to highly acidic urine, purine-rich diets, insulin resistance, and gout.
  • Struvite (Infection) Stones: Secondary to chronic urinary tract infections caused by urease-producing bacteria.
  • Cystine Stones: A rare genetic metabolic disorder of renal amino acid transport.

Common Myths About Dietary Calcium

A widespread misunderstanding is that people with calcium stones should stop consuming dietary calcium (such as milk, curd, or paneer). In reality, restricting dietary calcium actually increases stone risk!

When you consume adequate dietary calcium with meals, the calcium binds to dietary oxalate in the gut, forming an insoluble compound that is safely excreted in stool. If gut calcium is deficient, free oxalate is rapidly absorbed into the bloodstream and excreted into the urine, where it binds to urinary calcium and forms stones.

Actionable Steps to Prevent Recurrent Stones

Nephrology guidelines highlight three core preventive interventions:

  • Maintain Adequate Hydration: Aim for enough fluid intake to produce at least 2.0 to 2.5 liters of pale, clear urine every 24 hours. In hot, humid climates like Kerala, water losses through sweat increase dramatically, demanding higher daily fluid replenishment.
  • Moderate Animal Protein & Sodium: High dietary sodium increases calcium excretion into the urine (hypercalciuria), while excessive animal meat intake acidifies urine and elevates uric acid.
  • Citrate-Rich Fluids: Urinary citrate is a potent natural inhibitor that coats calcium crystals and prevents them from growing. Adding fresh lemon or lime juice to your daily water intake provides a natural citrate boost.

When to Seek a Nephrology Metabolic Workup

If you have experienced multiple stones, have stones in both kidneys, or have a family history of renal failure, ask your nephrologist for a 24-hour urine metabolic evaluation. Tailored medical therapy can effectively prevent future episodes and protect renal parenchyma.
